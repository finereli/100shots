Why people stop

Your first posts, sales calls and cold emails go badly. Everyone's do. But if you're used to being good at things, being bad at something feels like evidence about you. So you try it two or three times, decide it isn't for you, and stop. Usually you stop right before it would have started to work.

It's harder because you want the result. You want customers, an audience or a closed deal, and one more mediocre post doesn't feel any closer to that. You can't feel yourself making progress, so you start to "forget" to practise.

How the method works

Decide in advance that you'll be bad at it 100 times. Your first attempt is number 100, the next is 99, then 98. You move the number with every attempt, whether it went well or not.

You count the way you do before surgery, when the anesthetist asks you to count backwards from 20. You pay attention to the numbers instead of the fear, and you're under before you've had time to panic. Counting down from 100 helps in the same way with the dread before a sales call.

You probably won't need all 100. In Eli's experience, about 20 real attempts get you to basic competence at a founder skill like posting or selling. A hundred is there so that no single bad attempt comes close to using up your budget.

After each attempt, you write a line: what worked, and what you'll try next. You get better by learning from one attempt to the next, and you can't do that if you don't look back at them.

Founders like to say it's a numbers game. It is, and the numbers include the bad ones, so count those too.

Who it's for

  • Founders and indie hackers doing their own marketing and sales: posts, sales calls, cold DMs, launches.
  • Creators building an audience with videos, articles, newsletters or talks.
  • Practitioners levelling up, like someone who sells well one-on-one and is starting to run webinars.
  • Anyone learning a skill that improves with each try, such as drawing, an instrument or a language.

It fits less well when one more repetition doesn't make you better, as with eating well or going to bed on time. A regular habit tracker is fine for those.

The app

The app is one way to practise the method. Each skill you're working on is a card with a big number, the shots you have left out of 100. Tap the check after every attempt, good or bad. A sheet opens so you can write a quick note about how it went, or skip it. Each habit keeps its full history, with the notes.

The app doesn't count days. A week off costs you nothing, and five sales calls in one afternoon count as five. Your shots stay private. There's no feed and no profile, and nobody to confess a bad week to.

  • One card per skill, counting down from 100, with a dot for every shot (or a slim progress bar in the small-card view).
  • One tap per attempt, with Undo for mis-taps.
  • A note on every shot: a sheet after each one, or only when you ask. Read and edit any note later in the history.
  • Rename, delete and drag to reorder your habits.
  • An optional evening reminder ("Did you take any shots today?") that skips days you already took one.
  • Works offline, with your data on your device. Optional Google sign-in syncs it across devices.
  • Light and dark themes.

Questions

Is it free?
Yes. No ads, no trial, no paid plan.
Do I need an account?
No. Everything works on one device without signing in. Sign in with Google only if you want the same shots on your phone and your computer.
Which devices does it work on?
Any phone, tablet or computer with a modern browser. It's a web app you can install: on Android and desktop Chrome or Edge, use "Install app"; on iPhone and iPad, tap Share, then "Add to Home Screen". Once installed it opens like any other app and works offline.
What does it look like for sales calls?
Add a habit called "Sales calls". After each call, booked or not, tap the check, and the card goes from 100 to 99. In the note you write what you'd change, say "pitched too early, ask about their week first". Before the next call you read it in the history.
Why count backwards and not up?
Counting up has no end, so each bad attempt reads as a setback. When you count down from 100, the bad attempts are part of a plan you made in advance, and the falling number shows progress you can't feel yet.
Can't I just outsource this?
You can hand almost any part of a business to a freelancer or an AI now. Showing up on the call yourself, or posting under your own name, is the part you can't hand off. Eli tried outsourcing his marketing many times, and his business only grew when he was out there himself.
Does it have reminders?
Yes, one optional evening reminder per day, at a time you choose, skipped on days you already took a shot. On iPhone and iPad, reminders need the app added to the Home Screen (iOS 16.4 or later).
Where is my data stored?
On your device: each habit's name, the time of each shot and any notes you write. If you sign in with Google, a copy of the same data is kept on 100 Shots' server under your Google account id so your devices can sync; signing out syncs one last time and clears the device. If you turn on reminders, the server keeps your device's push address, the reminder time, your time zone and the date of your last shot, but not your habits or notes. Nothing is sold or shared, and there are no ads or trackers. The source is on GitHub if you want to check.
What doesn't it do?
It doesn't tell you what to post or say, grade your attempts, or draw charts. Nobody else sees your shots. It counts attempts and keeps your notes.
What happens after 100?
The card says "All 100 done" and keeps logging your shots. By then, what you were bad at has usually become something you just do.
How is this different from a streak tracker?
A streak tracker rewards consecutive days and punishes a missed one. 100 Shots counts attempts and ignores days. Missing a week costs nothing, and ten attempts in one day count as ten.
Who made it?
Eli Finer, an independent developer and founder coach. The method started as a post he wrote about counting attempts backwards, and he uses it himself: publishing apps is one of his habits, and this app was one of those shots. The source is on GitHub.

When to use something else

If you want detailed habit statistics, many daily habits on a schedule, or a streak to protect, a full habit tracker like Loop Habit Tracker, Habitify or Streaks fits better. If you track sets and weights at the gym, use a workout log. 100 Shots counts attempts at something you're still bad at, and keeps what you learn from each one.
